Everything the triangle teaches

Five short lessons — from the basic definition to where these numbers show up in daily life.

A triangular arrangement of sums

Pascal's Triangle is a triangular arrangement of numbers in which every number is the sum of the two numbers directly above it. The triangle starts with a single 1 at the top (row 0), and every row after that begins and ends with 1.


Because of how it's built, the triangle is perfectly symmetric — the left half mirrors the right half exactly. That symmetry isn't decorative: it reflects a deeper mathematical fact, that choosing "k items out of n" always matches choosing "n minus k items out of n".


Each full row of the triangle is also a complete row of binomial coefficients — the numbers you'd get from expanding (a + b)ⁿ.

Key features

  • Starts with 1 at the very top.
  • Every row begins and ends with 1.
  • Each inner number is the sum of the two above it.
  • The triangle is symmetric about its centre line.
  • Row n sums to exactly 2ⁿ.

The one rule behind every number

Every cell is simply the two cells sitting above it, added together. Apply that single rule over and over and the whole triangle builds itself.

Building it, one row at a time

  1. Start with 1Row 0 is just the number 1, sitting alone at the apex.
  2. Add the two numbers aboveEvery new number in the next row is the sum of the pair of numbers sitting diagonally above it. Any missing side is treated as 1.
  3. Close each row with 1Because there's only ever one number above the very first and very last position, each row naturally begins and ends with 1.
  4. RepeatKeep applying the same rule, row after row, for as many rows as you need — the pattern never runs out.

Coefficients of (a + b)ⁿ

Every row of Pascal's Triangle gives the coefficients you get from expanding a binomial raised to a power. Row n of the triangle (counting from 0) is exactly the coefficient list for (a + b)ⁿ.

(a + b)^4 = 1a^4 + 4a^3b + 6a^2b^2 + 4ab^3 + 1b^4 Coefficients used: 1 4 6 4 1 Pascal's row 4: 1 4 6 4 1 ✓ identical

This is why the triangle is so central to algebra: instead of multiplying out a binomial expansion by hand, you can simply read the coefficients straight off the matching row.

Named after Blaise Pascal

The triangle is named after the French mathematician and philosopher Blaise Pascal, who studied it closely and published a detailed treatise on it, Traité du triangle arithmétique, in 1653 — using it to formalise ideas in probability and combinatorics.


Interestingly, Pascal didn't discover the pattern first. Versions of the same triangle appear centuries earlier in Chinese, Persian, and Indian mathematics — but it was Pascal's systematic treatment that connected it clearly to probability theory, which is why his name stuck in the West.

Glossary

Terms worth knowing

Binomial
An algebraic expression with exactly two terms, such as (a + b).
Binomial coefficient
The number in front of each term in a binomial expansion — exactly the numbers found in Pascal's Triangle.
Binomial theorem
The rule describing how to expand (a + b)ⁿ into a sum of terms, using Pascal's Triangle for the coefficients.
Combinatorics
The branch of mathematics concerned with counting, arrangement, and combination of objects.
Combination (n choose k)
The number of ways to choose k items from a set of n items, regardless of order — each entry in Pascal's Triangle is one such value.
Row
One horizontal line of numbers in the triangle. Rows are numbered starting from 0 at the apex.
Symmetry
A property where the triangle looks identical when reflected across its central vertical line.
Coefficient
A numerical multiplier attached to a term in an algebraic expression.
Probability distribution
A description of how likely each possible outcome of a random event is; the binomial distribution draws directly on these coefficients.
Recursive pattern
A pattern where each new value is defined using previous values — exactly how each triangle entry depends on the row above it.
